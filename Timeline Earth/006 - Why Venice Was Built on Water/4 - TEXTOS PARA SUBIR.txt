This is Venice. A city of palaces, churches and squares, built in the middle of the sea.
There are no cars. The streets are canals, the buses are boats, and many houses rise straight out of the water.
And under all that stone, holding the whole city up, is something you would never expect: millions of wooden poles.
Building on land would have been so much easier. So why would anyone build a city on water?
By the end of this video, you'll see how fear, salt, wood and trade turned a swamp into the richest city in Europe, and why that city is still fighting the sea today.
Venice sits at the top of the Adriatic Sea, in the north-east of Italy. Rivers flowing down from the Alps have built a wide, flat coast here.
Between that coast and the open sea lies a huge, shallow lagoon, about fifty kilometers long. It's the largest wetland in the Mediterranean.
A long line of narrow sandy islands separates the lagoon from the sea. The tide flows in and out through just three openings.
The city itself is built on around one hundred and eighteen small islands, packed so closely together that from above they look like one.
Those islands are separated by around one hundred and fifty canals, and joined by more than four hundred bridges.
The biggest canal, the Grand Canal, snakes through the middle of the city in a giant S, about four kilometers long. For centuries, only one bridge crossed it: the Rialto.
Today, the historic city is only part of the municipality of Venice. Most of its residents actually live on the mainland, in places like Mestre, connected by a long bridge.
It's beautiful. But think about how strange this is. The ground is soft mud and sand. The water is salty. Fresh water, stone and wood all had to come from somewhere else.
No sensible planner would choose this place for a great city. And nobody did choose it. People were forced into it.
In Roman times, this region was rich and busy. The great city here was Aquileia, and nearby were towns like Padua and Altino. The lagoon was a place for fishermen.
But in the four hundreds, the Western Roman Empire was falling apart, and invaders crossed the Alps into northern Italy.
In 452, Attila and the Huns destroyed Aquileia. Many people from the mainland fled to the islands of the lagoon, where horsemen could not follow.
Most went back when the danger passed. But in 568, the Lombards invaded Italy and stayed. This time, many refugees stayed in the lagoon for good.
The lagoon was the perfect hiding place. The water is so shallow that big ships run aground, and the safe channels between the mudflats were known only to the locals.
At first, the most important settlement was Torcello, an island in the north of the lagoon. Its cathedral was founded in the year 639, and a later version of it still stands today.
That defense was tested around the year 810, when Pepin, son of Charlemagne, attacked with a Frankish army. His fleet got stuck in the shallows, and the attack failed.
Afterwards, the lagoon's government moved to the safest group of islands in the middle, around a place called Rivo Alto, the high bank. Today we call it Rialto.
So Venice wasn't built on water in spite of the danger. It was built on water because of the danger. The lagoon was its castle wall.
But how do you live in a swamp? The first lagoon people survived on two things the water gave them for free: fish, and salt.
Fresh water was a constant problem. Venetians collected rain under their squares, filtering it through layers of sand into underground cisterns. Thousands of wellheads still dot the city.
Salt was precious in a world without fridges, because it preserved food. Venetians harvested it in salt pans and traded it up the rivers of northern Italy. That was the start of their fortune.
As the settlements grew, the Venetians had to solve a huge engineering problem. How do you put heavy stone buildings on soft mud?
Their answer was to drive wooden piles deep into the mud, down to a firmer layer of compacted clay below. Then they built on top of them.
The piles were packed tightly together, sometimes many per square meter. Workers hammered them in by hand, one by one.
The great church of Santa Maria della Salute, finished in the 1680s, is said to stand on more than a million wooden piles.
So in a sense, Venice stands on a forest turned upside down.
The famous Rialto Bridge, finished in stone in 1591 after several wooden bridges, rests on thousands of wooden piles driven into the banks of the Grand Canal.
You'd expect wood under water to rot. But it doesn't, because buried in mud and salt water, there's almost no oxygen. The microbes that rot wood can't survive.
Over centuries, minerals from the water even seeped into the wood, making it harder. Some piles have held up buildings for many hundreds of years.
When the bell tower of St. Mark's suddenly collapsed in 1902, the city rebuilt it exactly as before. The motto was: where it was, as it was. It reopened in 1912.
On top of the piles came a platform of wood, then foundations of Istrian stone, a dense white limestone brought by ship from across the Adriatic. It barely lets water through.
All that wood and stone had to come from far away. Timber was floated down the rivers from the forests of the Alps and the hills of the mainland.
Venice protected its forests carefully, because without wood there would be no new buildings, and no ships. And ships were about to make Venice very rich.
The lagoon gave Venice something else: there was almost no farmland. Venetians couldn't live from the land, so they lived from the sea.
Venice sat at the northern tip of the Adriatic, the closest sea port to the rich cities of central Europe. Goods from the east could be shipped here, then carried over the Alps.
In 1082, the Byzantine emperor gave Venetian merchants special trading rights across his empire, in exchange for help against his enemies. Venice's ships soon dominated trade with Constantinople.
Venetian galleys sailed to Constantinople, to Alexandria in Egypt, and to the ports of the Levant, bringing back spices, silk, cotton and sugar.
In 1204, Venice provided the ships for the Fourth Crusade, which ended up attacking Constantinople. Venice took a large share of the spoils, including ports and islands across the eastern Mediterranean.
At its height, Venice ruled a chain of colonies along the coast of Dalmatia, in Greece, and on large islands like Crete and Cyprus. A maritime empire run from a lagoon.
Its ships came from the Arsenal, a giant state shipyard. Thousands of workers built galleys in stages, almost like an assembly line, centuries before modern factories.
In 1574, to impress the visiting king of France, the Arsenal is said to have assembled a complete galley during the time he spent at a banquet.
Venetians traveled far. Marco Polo, a Venetian merchant, journeyed to China in the late twelve hundreds, and his book made Europe dream of the riches of Asia.
To protect the city from fire, glassmakers were moved to the island of Murano in 1291. Their techniques were closely guarded secrets, and Murano glass is still famous today.
Trade also brought disease. Venice set up some of the first permanent quarantine stations on islands in the lagoon. Ships had to wait there for up to forty days, in Venetian, quaranta giorni.
Around 1500, Venice was also one of the leading printing centers in Europe. Printers like Aldus Manutius published Greek and Latin classics in small, affordable books.
The city was also a republic, ruled by an elected leader called the doge and a council of wealthy families. It lasted for about a thousand years.
Every year, on Ascension Day, the doge sailed out to the open sea in a golden ship and threw a ring into the water, symbolically marrying Venice to the sea.
With the money came the palaces along the Grand Canal, the Doge's Palace and the golden mosaics of St. Mark's Basilica. Around 1500, Venice was one of the biggest and richest cities in Europe.

But there was a hidden problem. A lagoon is not permanent. Left alone, it slowly disappears.
Rivers bring mud and sand, which slowly fill the lagoon until it turns into marsh and dry land. The sea, on the other hand, can break through the sandbars and turn it into an open bay.
Either way, Venice would lose its protective water. So for centuries, the Republic fought to keep the lagoon exactly as it was.
In the 1500s and 1600s, Venetian engineers dug new channels to divert the rivers Brenta, Piave and Sile away from the lagoon, so that their mud would flow straight into the sea instead.
In 1604, they even moved part of the mouth of the Po, Italy's longest river, further south, to stop its sediment from drifting toward the lagoon.
In the 1700s, they built the murazzi, massive stone sea walls along the thin sandbars, to stop storm waves from breaking through.
A special magistrate for the waters watched over the whole system. Damaging the lagoon could be punished severely.
So the lagoon you see today is not entirely natural. It has been shaped and protected by people for centuries.
Meanwhile, the world was changing. After 1498, when Portuguese sailors reached India around Africa, more of the spice trade moved to the Atlantic.
Venice slowly lost its power. In 1797, Napoleon's army arrived, and the Republic, after about a thousand years, came to an end.
Napoleon even had the bronze horses of St. Mark's taken to Paris. They only came back to Venice in 1815, after his defeat.
Later, Venice was finally tied to the mainland. A railway bridge opened in 1846, and a road bridge in 1933. The island city was no longer an island.
Today, Venice faces a new version of its oldest enemy. The water is rising, and the city is sinking.
Several times a year, high tides push water into the city. Venetians call it acqua alta, high water.
It happens when several things combine: a high tide, low air pressure, and a strong south-east wind, the scirocco, pushing water up the long, narrow Adriatic.
St. Mark's Square is one of the lowest parts of the city, so it is often the first place to flood. People cross it on raised wooden walkways.
The worst flood on record came in November 1966, when the water reached about one hundred and ninety-four centimeters above normal. In November 2019, it reached about one hundred and eighty-seven.
At the same time, the land has been sinking. In the twentieth century, pumping groundwater for industry on the mainland made the ground compact. The pumping was stopped in the 1970s.
Add rising sea levels, and Venice has lost around twenty-five centimeters of height relative to the sea since 1900.
So Italy built a new kind of defense, at the same three inlets where the tide enters the lagoon. It's called MOSE.
MOSE is made of seventy-eight steel gates lying on the seabed. When a dangerous tide is forecast, air is pumped in, and the gates rise to block the sea.
The project took decades and cost billions of euros. The gates were raised for the first time in October 2020, and that day, St. Mark's Square stayed dry.
But there's a catch. Each time the gates close, the lagoon is cut off from the sea. If sea levels keep rising, they may need to close so often that the lagoon itself suffers.
There's another threat, and it's not the sea. In the 1950s, around one hundred and seventy-five thousand people lived in the historic city. Today it's fewer than fifty thousand.
Living on the water is expensive and difficult. Homes are hard to maintain, everything must be carried by boat, and many apartments have become holiday rentals.
Meanwhile, millions of tourists visit every year, many only for the day. In 2024, Venice became the first city to charge day-trippers an entry fee on its busiest days.
In the 1500s, thousands of gondolas moved people around the city. Today there are only a few hundred, mostly carrying visitors. Each one is built slightly asymmetric, so that a single oarsman standing on one side can row it in a straight line.
And yet, for those who stay, Venice offers something rare: it's one of the largest car-free cities in the world. Children walk to school over bridges, and the only traffic jams are on the canals.
Huge cruise ships, once a common sight sailing past St. Mark's, were banned from the city center in 2021.
Since 1987, Venice and its lagoon have been a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Not just the buildings, but the lagoon itself.
Because that's the real lesson of Venice. The city and the water are not enemies. Without the lagoon, Venice would never have existed.
So let's put the whole story together.
Venice was built on water because the lagoon was the safest place in a dangerous world. Invaders on horseback, and even large fleets, could not reach it.
Millions of wooden piles made it possible to build in stone. And with no farmland, the sea became the city's road to wealth.
For fifteen hundred years, Venice has survived by understanding the water around it. Whether it survives the next century depends on whether we still can.

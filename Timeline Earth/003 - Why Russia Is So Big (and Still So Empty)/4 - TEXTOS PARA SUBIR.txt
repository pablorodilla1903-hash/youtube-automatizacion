This is Russia. The largest country on Earth, by far.
It covers more than seventeen million square kilometers. That's more than the entire surface of the dwarf planet Pluto.
And yet Bangladesh, a country more than one hundred times smaller, has more people.
Most of this giant is almost empty. Forests, frozen ground and rivers, for thousands of kilometers, with hardly a town in sight.
So how did one country end up with so much land, and why, after four centuries, is so much of it still empty?
By the end of this video, you'll see how the cold, rivers that flow the wrong way, a band of fur hunters and one very long railway shaped the map of Russia.
First, the size. Russia is almost twice as big as Canada, the second largest country, and about as big as the United States and Australia put together.
Altogether, Russia holds more than a tenth of all the land on our planet.
It is so wide that it stretches across eleven time zones. When people in Moscow are going to bed, people in Kamchatka, on the Pacific, are already getting up.
From the Baltic Sea in the west to the Bering Strait in the east, Russia stretches about nine thousand kilometers. It touches two continents and three oceans.
It shares land borders with fourteen countries, from Norway and Finland in the north-west to China and North Korea in the far east.
And at its eastern tip, Russia is only about eighty kilometers from Alaska, across the Bering Strait. Remember that, because it becomes important later.
So on paper, Russia has room for almost anyone. Room for cities, for farms, for hundreds of millions of people.
But today it has around one hundred and forty-five million. Spread evenly, that's fewer than nine people per square kilometer.
For comparison, Germany has more than two hundred people per square kilometer, and even the United States has around thirty-five.
But averages hide the real story. Because in Russia, people are not spread evenly at all.
Look at this line. The Ural Mountains run about two and a half thousand kilometers from the Arctic Ocean towards Kazakhstan.
They are old and fairly low. The highest peak is under two thousand meters. But for centuries geographers have used them as the line between Europe and Asia.
West of the Urals is European Russia. East of them is Asian Russia: Siberia and the Far East. The difference between the two is enormous.
Roughly three out of every four Russians live in the European part, which is only about a quarter of the country.
That's where the big cities are. Moscow, Saint Petersburg, Nizhny Novgorod, Kazan. All of them west of the mountains.
Moscow alone has about thirteen million people. That's more than all of the Russian Far East put together, a region more than twice the size of India.
And east of the Urals, people cluster in a thin line along the southern edge of Siberia, close to the borders with Kazakhstan, Mongolia and China.
Yekaterinburg, Omsk, Novosibirsk, Krasnoyarsk, Irkutsk. Almost every big Siberian city sits on that one southern line.
North of it, there are areas larger than France with fewer people than a small town.
Siberia alone covers around thirteen million square kilometers. If it were a country, it would still be the largest country on Earth.
So the real question isn't why Russia has so few people. It's why so few of them live in three quarters of their own country.
The first answer is the one you'd expect. The cold. But Siberian cold is on a level most of us can't imagine.
In the village of Oymyakon, in Yakutia, a temperature of around minus sixty-eight degrees Celsius has been recorded. It's often called the coldest permanently inhabited place on Earth.
Not far away is Yakutsk, a city of more than three hundred thousand people, where the average temperature in January is below minus thirty-five.
Siberia is so cold because it's so far from the ocean. Water stores heat, and far inland there is nothing to soften the winter.
The warm Atlantic air that keeps Western Europe mild loses its strength as it travels east. By the time it reaches Siberia, there's almost nothing left. And the Arctic is wide open to the north.
The cold has a second, hidden effect. Around two thirds of Russia sits on permafrost, ground that stays frozen all year round, sometimes hundreds of meters deep.
Building on it is a nightmare. A heated house slowly melts the ground beneath it, and the building starts to tilt and crack.
So in cities like Yakutsk, buildings stand on concrete piles driven deep into the frozen ground, with a gap underneath so that cold air can flow below the floor.
Then there are the rivers. Siberia has some of the largest rivers on Earth: the Ob, the Yenisey and the Lena. But look which way they flow.
North. Into the Arctic Ocean, which is frozen for much of the year. Not towards any warm sea, and not towards any big market.
Worse, in spring the southern parts of these rivers melt first, while their mouths in the north are still frozen. The water has nowhere to go, and it floods huge areas.
In summer, the thawed surface turns into swamp. Western Siberia holds some of the largest wetlands in the world.
And over all of it grows the taiga, the largest forest on Earth. Russia alone holds about a fifth of all the world's forests.
Further south, the soil is better, but the growing season is short. In much of Siberia, frost-free summers last only around three months.
Russia's best farmland, the famous black earth, lies in the south-west, near the European part. And people, as always, live where food can be grown.
So if Siberia is this hard to live in, why does it belong to Russia at all? To answer that, we need to go back five hundred years, to a much smaller country.
In the thirteenth century, the Mongols conquered the lands of the Rus. For around two hundred years, the Russian princes paid tribute to the Golden Horde.
Moscow grew powerful as the collector of that tribute. And in 1480, under Ivan the Third, it stopped paying for good.
At that time, Moscow's state was a fraction of today's Russia. A land of forests and rivers in the west, far from any warm sea.
Then, in 1552, Ivan the Fourth, known as Ivan the Terrible, took Kazan on the Volga. A few years later he took Astrakhan, at the river's mouth on the Caspian Sea.
Now the whole Volga, Europe's longest river, was in Russian hands. And the road to the east lay open.
What pulled the Russians east was not farmland. It was fur. Sable, fox and ermine were worth a fortune in Europe and Asia, and Siberia's forests were full of them.
Around 1581, a Cossack leader called Yermak, paid by the wealthy Stroganov merchant family, crossed the Urals with a few hundred men.
In 1582 they captured Qashliq, the capital of the Khanate of Sibir, near the modern city of Tobolsk. The name of that small khanate would one day cover the whole region: Siberia.
Yermak died a few years later. But the door he opened never closed again.
What followed was one of the fastest expansions in history. Small groups of Cossacks and fur hunters moved from river to river, carrying their boats over the short gaps between them.
Each time, they built a wooden fort. Tobolsk in 1587. Tomsk in 1604. Yakutsk in 1632. Many of those forts are still the region's cities today.
In 1639, Russian explorers reached the Pacific Ocean, at the Sea of Okhotsk. From the Urals to the Pacific had taken them less than sixty years.
The system was simple. The forts collected a tribute in furs, called yasak, from the native peoples of Siberia. The furs went to Moscow, and paid for the next push east.
And here's the key. The Russians didn't need many people to claim Siberia. A few forts, a few hundred men each, were enough to hold territory the size of countries.
That's the secret of Russia's map. It was claimed by a handful of hunters, not settled by millions of farmers. The land came first. The people never really followed.

But fur alone doesn't explain why Russia kept growing for centuries. For that, look at the land around Moscow. It's flat. Almost perfectly flat, for thousands of kilometers.
There are no high mountains and no wide seas to protect the heart of the country. Armies can march straight in from almost any direction.
And they did. The Mongols in the thirteenth century. A Polish army that occupied Moscow in 1610. Sweden in 1708. Napoleon in 1812. Germany in 1941.
Napoleon reached Moscow in 1812 with a huge army. But Russia was so big that he couldn't force a surrender. The city burned, winter came, and his army was destroyed on the long road home.
Russian leaders drew a lesson from this. If you can't build walls, build distance. The more land between the enemy and Moscow, the safer Moscow is.
Historians call this strategic depth. And it helps explain why the Russian Empire kept pushing its borders outward, towards seas, mountains and deserts that could act as walls.
Peter the Great fought his way to the Baltic and founded Saint Petersburg in 1703. Later, the empire reached the Black Sea, the Caucasus mountains and the deserts of Central Asia.
In the east, the empire crossed the sea to Alaska. In the eighteen hundreds, Russian America even had trading posts as far south as California.
But Alaska was even harder to hold than Siberia. It was far from Moscow, very expensive to supply, and the fur animals were being hunted out.
So in 1867, Russia sold Alaska to the United States for seven point two million dollars. That's roughly two cents per acre.
It's one of the few times Russia ever gave land away. And it shows the problem perfectly: claiming a territory is one thing, but filling it with people is something else entirely.
By the late eighteen hundreds, the empire had a serious problem. Siberia was enormous, rich in resources, and almost impossible to reach.
Travel from Moscow to the Pacific could take months, by river boat in summer and by horse-drawn sledge in winter.
So in 1891, Russia began building the Trans-Siberian Railway, from Moscow all the way to Vladivostok on the Pacific.
The main line is about nine thousand three hundred kilometers long. It's still the longest railway line in the world, and the full journey takes about a week.
Crews laid track across swamps and permafrost, and around Lake Baikal they had to blast tunnels along the cliffs. The last section, along the Amur River, was only finished in 1916.
Lake Baikal, by the way, is the deepest lake on Earth, more than one and a half kilometers deep. It holds around a fifth of the world's unfrozen fresh surface water.
The railway did what the forts never could. It brought people. Millions of peasants moved east, and new cities grew along the line.
Novosibirsk was founded in 1893, where the railway crossed the Ob River. Today it's Russia's third largest city, with around one and a half million people.
But notice where the railway runs: along the far south of Siberia, where the climate is mildest. So the settlers stayed there too, which is exactly why Siberia's cities form that thin southern line today.
In the twentieth century, the Soviet Union pushed into the north as well. It wanted the metals, the coal, the oil and the gas buried under the frozen ground.
Many northern towns were first built with forced labor from the Gulag camps. Later, the state offered higher wages and extra holidays to attract workers to the north.
Norilsk, above the Arctic Circle, became one of the northernmost large cities in the world, with more than one hundred and seventy thousand people, built around huge nickel mines.
For a few decades, it looked like Siberia might finally fill up. But that growth depended on one thing: the state paying for it.
In 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed. The subsidies, the northern bonuses and the cheap flights to the rest of the country largely disappeared.
And people left. Chukotka, the region facing Alaska, lost around two thirds of its population in little more than a decade.
Across the whole Russian Far East, the population fell by roughly a fifth after 1991, as people moved back west, towards Moscow and the warmer south.
The government has tried to reverse it. Since 2016, any Russian citizen can apply for a free hectare of land in the Far East. Few people have actually moved there for it.
Yet Siberia is far from useless. Much of Russia's oil and natural gas comes from beneath Western Siberia and the Arctic, and those exports pay for a large part of the country's budget.
But modern oil and gas fields don't need big cities. Workers often fly in for a few weeks, then fly home again. The wealth leaves, and so do the people.
And now the climate is changing. Siberia is warming faster than most of the planet, and the permafrost under some towns is starting to thaw.
That could open new farmland and longer shipping seasons along the Arctic coast. But it also threatens roads, pipelines and buildings that were designed for ground that never melts.
So even with a warmer climate, Siberia is unlikely to fill up any time soon.
So let's put the whole story together.
Russia is so big because a small state on an open plain kept pushing outward for protection, and because fur made it worth racing across Siberia, all the way to the Pacific.
And it's so empty because most of that land is frozen, far from any warm sea, crossed by rivers flowing into the Arctic, with summers too short for most farming.
The land was claimed by hunters, connected by one railway, and settled only along its southern edge. Four hundred years later, most Russians still live where the story began: in the west.

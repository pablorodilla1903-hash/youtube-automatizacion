This is Canada, the second largest country on Earth.
And yet it has roughly the same number of people as the state of California.
In fact, Canada only passed forty million people in 2023. If the country were a huge house, almost everyone would be standing in one narrow hallway.
Even stranger, most of those people live squeezed into a thin strip right next to the United States.
So why does almost everyone in a giant country live along one edge of it?
By the end of this video, you'll see how the cold, a rocky shield, two rivers and one railway drew this line, and why the United States keeps pulling Canadians south.
Canada covers almost ten million square kilometers. That's bigger than the whole United States, and more than twenty times the size of Spain.
It's so wide that it stretches across six time zones. When it's breakfast time in Vancouver, people in Newfoundland are already thinking about lunch.
It also has more lakes than any other country on Earth, and one of the longest coastlines in the world. So on paper, there's plenty of room for everyone.
But spread evenly, that would be only about four people for every square kilometer.
In reality, they are not spread evenly at all. Roughly two out of every three Canadians live within about one hundred kilometers of the US border.
Look at the biggest cities. Vancouver, Calgary, Winnipeg, Toronto, Montreal. All of them sit in the south.
Toronto's metropolitan area has around seven million people. Montreal has over four million, and Vancouver close to three. Just these three cities hold about a third of the whole country.
Toronto is actually much closer to New York and Detroit than to Vancouver. Many Canadian cities have more in common with their American neighbors to the south than with other Canadian cities thousands of kilometers away.
And Canada isn't unique. Australians crowd onto their coasts, and most Russians live in the west of their country. Giant countries are often mostly empty.
Meanwhile the northern territory of Nunavut is bigger than Mexico, and home to only around forty thousand people.
From space at night, the pattern is obvious. Canada looks like a thin string of lights along its southern edge, with almost total darkness above it, stretching all the way to the Arctic Ocean.
The first reason is simple. Most of Canada is extremely cold.
In January, Toronto averages a few degrees below zero. Yellowknife and Iqaluit, further north, sit at around minus twenty-five.
In much of the north, snow can cover the ground for more than half of the year, and Hudson Bay, a sea bigger than many countries, freezes over every winter.
Even the southern prairies have brutal winters. Winnipeg is one of the coldest big cities in the world, and locals sometimes call it Winterpeg. Now imagine a thousand kilometers further north.
That leaves a very short growing season. In the far south, farmers have several frost free months. Across much of the north, there are only a few weeks, far too little for most crops.
Under around half of the country, the ground is permafrost. It stays frozen all year, which makes building roads, pipes and houses difficult and expensive.
Further north you cross the tree line. Beyond it, the forest disappears and the tundra begins: low shrubs, moss and rock, frozen for most of the year.
Then there's the rock. The Canadian Shield, a huge block of ancient rock covered by thin soil, lakes and forest, spreads across about half of the country.
And in summer, much of the Shield turns into lakes, bogs and muskeg, soft wet ground full of insects. For centuries, the easiest way to cross it was not by land at all, but by canoe.
Put the cold and the rock together, and only around five percent of Canada's land is good for growing crops.
Now compare the west coast. Vancouver averages several degrees above zero in January, because warm, wet air from the Pacific softens the winter. The mountains behind it block the Arctic cold.
And people have always lived where food can be grown. Which in Canada means one place: the far south.
But cold and rock only explain half the story. The other half is water. Look at the St. Lawrence River and the Great Lakes.
Long before Europeans arrived, Indigenous peoples had lived across this land for thousands of years, from the Pacific coast to the Arctic. And their settlements already followed the same logic: people gathered where food was reliable.
On the Pacific coast, rich salmon runs supported large villages. Around the Great Lakes, the Haudenosaunee farmed corn, beans and squash, the famous three sisters. In the Arctic, the Inuit hunted seals, whales and caribou.
The Great Lakes hold about a fifth of all the fresh surface water on the planet, and they are connected to the Atlantic by the St. Lawrence.
When French explorers arrived, this river was a highway into the heart of the continent. Quebec City was founded in 1608, and Montreal in 1642, right on its banks.
Montreal's location was no accident either. Just upstream, the Lachine Rapids stopped ships from going further, so goods had to be unloaded there. A city grew right at the point where the water route broke.
The fur trade followed the water too. Beaver pelts were carried by canoe along rivers and lakes, and trading posts grew into towns.
In 1670, the English king granted the Hudson's Bay Company control over all the land draining into Hudson Bay. It became one of the oldest companies in the world, and for two centuries it ruled a territory larger than Europe.
But even that empire was mostly a network of trading posts, not towns. The furs came from the north, while the money, the ships and the people stayed along the coasts and rivers.
Water also meant milder weather. Big lakes store heat in summer and release it in winter, so the land around them is warmer and greener than the interior.
Water meant transport, trade and milder winters than the interior. So the first Canada grew as a chain of towns along the St. Lawrence and the Great Lakes.
On the Atlantic coast, the story was similar. Fishing villages grew along the shores of Newfoundland and Nova Scotia, close to the rich cod grounds of the Grand Banks. People lived on the edge of the land, facing the water.
That's also why French is still spoken along the St. Lawrence today. The colony of New France grew along the river, and the language stayed where the first settlers lived.
Much later, in 1959, the St. Lawrence Seaway opened, allowing ocean ships to sail all the way into the Great Lakes, and turning these lakeside cities into ports.
That pattern never went away. Today, around half of all Canadians live in the corridor between Quebec City and Windsor, along that same water.

Further west, the border itself was drawn with a ruler. In 1818, Britain and the United States agreed on the forty-ninth parallel, and in 1846 the line was extended to the Pacific.
Including Alaska, the Canada US border runs for almost nine thousand kilometers, the longest border between two countries in the world.
But a line on a map doesn't make people move west. For that, Canada needed a railway.
In 1871, British Columbia agreed to join Canada on one condition: a railway linking it to the rest of the country. Without it, the west might have drifted toward the United States.
In 1885, the Canadian Pacific Railway was completed, linking Montreal to the Pacific coast. And it was built close to the border, partly to keep the western lands firmly Canadian.
Building it was brutally hard. Crews blasted tunnels through the Rocky Mountains, and thousands of Chinese workers took on some of the most dangerous sections through British Columbia.
On November 7, 1885, the last spike was driven at Craigellachie, in British Columbia. For the first time, you could cross Canada by land from ocean to ocean.
Towns grew wherever the trains stopped. Winnipeg, Regina, Calgary and Vancouver all boomed along the line, and they are still Canada's western capitals today.
Decades later, the Trans-Canada Highway followed the same logic. It runs across the south of the country, linking the same chain of cities the railway created.
To fill the prairies, the government offered land almost for free. Under the Dominion Lands Act of 1872, a settler could claim about one hundred sixty acres for a registration fee of just ten dollars.
Hundreds of thousands of settlers came, many from Europe. The prairies became one of the great wheat regions of the world. But again, the farms stayed in the south, where the growing season is long enough.
The north had its own boom. In 1896, gold was found in the Klondike, in the Yukon. Tens of thousands of people rushed north, and Dawson City grew into a town of many thousands almost overnight.
Within a few years the gold ran low and most people left. Today Dawson City is home to only a couple of thousand people. In the north, booms come and go. The south stays.
And then there is the neighbor. The United States has more than eight times Canada's population and the biggest economy in the world.
Around three quarters of everything Canada exports goes to the United States.
Highways, railways, bridges and pipelines all run north to south across the border, and the cities closest to it are the ones best placed to trade.
The Ambassador Bridge between Windsor and Detroit carries more trade than almost any other crossing between the two countries, and Toronto's Highway 401 is one of the busiest highways in North America.
Every new business, factory or warehouse chooses the same places, because that's where the customers, the workers and the transport already are. Growth attracts more growth.
Immigration reinforces the pattern. Most of Canada's population growth now comes from newcomers, and most of them settle in the big southern cities, where jobs and communities already exist.
Notice that the biggest cities also sit on water. Toronto on Lake Ontario, Montreal on an island in the St. Lawrence, Vancouver on the Pacific. The border, the water and the warmth all line up in the same places.
Here's a fun fact. Canada's southernmost tip, near Point Pelee in Lake Erie, is further south than the northern border of California.
The north isn't completely empty, though. Mines, oil sands and small towns exist there. But they are islands of people in a vast, frozen landscape.
So what is life like on the other side of the line? The Inuit have lived in the Arctic for around a thousand years, and people before them for thousands more.
In 1999, Canada created a new territory, Nunavut, which means our land in Inuktitut. Its capital, Iqaluit, is home to only around seven thousand people.
Even the name Iqaluit tells you how people survived there. In Inuktitut, it means roughly place of many fish.
Most communities in Nunavut can't be reached by road. There are no highways connecting them to the south, or even to each other.
In some places, roads only exist in winter. Ice roads are built across frozen lakes and muskeg, and trucks carry supplies to remote mines for just a few weeks before the ice melts again.
Food and fuel arrive by plane all year, and by ship during the short summer, when sea ice melts enough for the annual sealift to reach the coast.
That makes everyday things far more expensive. A basket of groceries in the far north can cost two or three times what it costs in Toronto.
Building is a challenge too. Houses often stand on stilts or piles, so their heat doesn't melt the frozen ground beneath them and make them sink.
And yet the north matters enormously to Canada. It holds diamond, gold and other mineral mines, huge amounts of fresh water, and Canada's claim to the Arctic itself.
Could this map change? The Arctic is warming several times faster than the rest of the planet.
As the sea ice shrinks, the Northwest Passage through Canada's Arctic islands is open for longer each summer. The explorer Roald Amundsen needed three years to sail through it, from 1903 to 1906.
The Arctic islands are huge. Baffin Island alone is one of the five largest islands on Earth, bigger than Spain, yet it's home to only around thirteen thousand people.
A warmer north could bring longer growing seasons, new shipping routes and new mines. But melting permafrost also cracks roads, runways and buildings that were designed for frozen ground.
Some scientists expect parts of the north to become better for farming as the climate warms. But good soil takes thousands of years to form, and much of the north is still bare rock.
So even if the climate changes, moving millions of people north would take generations of new roads, homes and cities. For now, the line is not going anywhere.
So let's put the whole story together.
The north is cold, frozen and rocky. The best soil and the mildest climate are in the far south. And the rivers and lakes that carried the first settlers run right along the border.
Then the railway that built the west was placed right next to that border. And the largest economy on Earth sits just on the other side of it.
That's why most Canadians live near the US border. Not because of one decision, but because climate, geography and history all pointed to the same thin strip of land.

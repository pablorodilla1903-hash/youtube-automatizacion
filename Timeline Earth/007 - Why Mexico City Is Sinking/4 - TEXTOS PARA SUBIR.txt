This is Mexico City. With more than twenty million people in its metropolitan area, it's one of the biggest cities on Earth.
And it's sinking. Not by a little. In some neighborhoods, the ground drops by tens of centimeters every single year.
Churches lean. Streets crack. Old buildings that once stood at street level now sit below it, or rise above it.
The strangest part? Five hundred years ago, most of this ground was underwater.
So why is Mexico City sinking?
By the end of this video, you'll see how an island city, a drained lake, a layer of soft clay and millions of thirsty people are pulling the ground down.
Around twenty-two million people live in the Mexico City metropolitan area. That's more than the whole population of countries like Chile or the Netherlands.
And it sits high up. The city center is about two thousand two hundred and forty meters above sea level, higher than many ski resorts in the Alps.
At that altitude, the air is thinner. Each breath holds roughly a quarter less oxygen than at sea level, something visiting athletes notice immediately.
It lies in a high valley, the Valley of Mexico, surrounded on all sides by mountains and volcanoes.
To the south-east rises Popocatépetl, an active volcano more than five thousand four hundred meters high. On clear days, you can see it from the city.
Here's the key fact. This valley is a closed basin. No river naturally drains it to the sea. Rain that falls here has nowhere to go.
So for thousands of years, water collected at the bottom of the valley, forming a chain of shallow lakes. And as rivers washed in fine mud, it settled on the lake floor.
Over thousands of years, that mud built up into a thick layer of very soft clay. Remember that clay. It's the hidden villain of this story.
Long before the Aztecs, this valley was already home to great cities. Teotihuacan, north-east of the lakes, was one of the largest cities in the Americas around the year 500.
There wasn't just one lake. A chain of connected lakes ran across the valley floor. The northern lakes were salty, while the southern ones, like Xochimilco and Chalco, were fed by springs and had fresher water.
When the Spanish arrived in 1519, the largest of these lakes, Lake Texcoco, covered much of the valley floor. And in the middle of it was a city.
According to tradition, the Mexica, the people we often call the Aztecs, founded their city on a small island in the lake around 1325.
Their legend says the gods told them to settle where they saw an eagle on a cactus, eating a snake. That image is still in the center of Mexico's flag today.
The island was a smart choice. It was easy to defend, and canoes could carry goods across the lake from every town on its shores.
Its great market, at Tlatelolco, amazed the Spanish. Their chroniclers described tens of thousands of people buying and selling there on a single day.
One Spanish soldier, Bernal Díaz del Castillo, later wrote that when they first saw the city rising from the water, some of them wondered if they were dreaming.
To grow food, they built chinampas: narrow artificial islands made of mud and plants, piled up in the shallow water and anchored by trees.
Chinampas were incredibly productive, with water always at hand. Some still exist today in Xochimilco, in the south of the city, and they are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
The city was connected to the shore by long causeways built across the water, with bridges that could be removed in case of attack.
Part of the lake was salty. So the Aztecs built a long dike, more than ten kilometers long, to separate fresher water around the city from the salty water to the east.
The dike was built in the mid fourteen hundreds, after a disastrous flood, and its design is traditionally credited to Nezahualcoyotl, the poet-king of the nearby city of Texcoco.
Fresh drinking water came by aqueduct from the springs of Chapultepec, on the western shore.
By 1519, Tenochtitlan may have had around two hundred thousand people, making it one of the largest cities in the world at the time.
The Aztecs lived with the lake. They built on it, farmed on it, and traveled across it. What came next would do the opposite.
In 1521, after a long siege, Spanish forces led by Hernán Cortés and their Indigenous allies conquered Tenochtitlan. The city was largely destroyed.
The Spanish built their new capital right on top of the ruins. But they built it like a European city: streets instead of canals, carts instead of canoes.
The dikes and canals that controlled the water fell apart. And during heavy rainy seasons, the lake rose and flooded the city.
The worst flood began in 1629. Parts of the city stayed underwater for about five years.
So the colonial government decided the lake was the enemy. If the valley had no exit, they would make one.
After the great flood, there were even serious discussions about moving the capital to higher ground. In the end, the city stayed, and chose to fight the water instead.
Starting in 1607, workers dug a tunnel, and later a huge open cut, through the hills in the north of the valley, to let water flow out toward the Gulf of Mexico.
Drainage went on for centuries. In 1900, the Gran Canal was completed. Then, from 1975, deep drainage tunnels were dug far beneath the city.
The first tunnel was designed by an engineer called Enrico Martínez. It often collapsed and clogged, and it took generations of work to turn it into a working drain.
The Gran Canal, finished under President Porfirio Díaz, ran for about forty-seven kilometers to carry the valley's water and sewage north.
Little by little, Lake Texcoco disappeared. Today, only small remnants of the old lake system are left.
On the dry lakebed, the city grew and grew, especially in the twentieth century, when millions of people moved here from the countryside.
In 1900, the city had around half a million people. By 2020, the metropolitan area had more than twenty-one million.
And all those people needed water. The lake was gone. So the city looked down, under its own feet.
If you're enjoying this, subscribe. Every week we find the story hidden in another map.
Under the valley lies an aquifer: layers of sand and gravel soaked with water, like a giant underground sponge.
From the late eighteen hundreds, and especially in the twentieth century, the city drilled thousands of wells into it.
As the water table dropped, wells had to be drilled deeper and deeper, often hundreds of meters down, to keep reaching the water.
Today, most of the city's water still comes from wells, pumped from the aquifer beneath it. Estimates usually put it at around six out of every ten liters.
The problem is balance. Every year, much more water is pumped out than rain can put back in.
And the city makes refilling harder. Concrete and asphalt cover the old lakebed, so rain that once soaked into the ground now runs into the drains.
Now remember that clay. The old lakebed clay is incredibly soft, and it's full of water. In places, it's more water than solid.
When water is pumped from the layers below, water drains out of the clay too. And when the clay loses its water, it shrinks and compacts. The ground on top sinks.
Think of a wet sponge under a heavy book. Squeeze the water out, and the sponge gets thinner. Except this sponge is the ground beneath twenty million people.
And here's the worst part. Once the clay is compacted, it doesn't spring back. Even if the aquifer refilled, most of the lost height would never return.
Scientists noticed the sinking more than a century ago. By the 1950s, engineers had clearly linked it to the pumping of groundwater.
So how much has it sunk? Since the early twentieth century, some parts of the city have dropped by around ten meters. That's the height of a three-story building.
Satellites now measure it with great precision. In the fastest-sinking areas, the ground is dropping by up to around thirty to forty centimeters a year.
The measurements come from satellite radar, which can detect changes in the height of the ground of just millimeters, from hundreds of kilometers up in space.
For comparison, global sea level is rising by a few millimeters a year. Parts of Mexico City are sinking roughly a hundred times faster.
You can see it in the city's most famous monument. The Angel of Independence, built in 1910, stands on deep foundations. So as the street around it sank, the column seemed to rise.
To reach its base, fourteen extra steps have had to be added over the years.
In some places, metal well casings anchored deep in firm ground now stick out of the pavement, because the ground around them has dropped away.
The Palace of Fine Arts, a huge building clad in heavy marble, has sunk noticeably since it was built. You can see that its entrance sits lower than the streets around it.
The Metropolitan Cathedral, on the main square, began leaning as different parts of its foundation sank at different speeds. In the 1990s, engineers carefully removed soil from under the higher parts to straighten it.
That's the real danger: uneven sinking. When one side of a building drops faster than the other, walls crack and floors tilt.
Water pipes break as the ground shifts under them. Roads crack. And the metro and drainage tunnels must constantly be repaired.
The soft clay also amplifies earthquakes. Seismic waves can shake much harder on the old lakebed than on solid rock just a few kilometers away.
And the sinking isn't the same everywhere. It's fastest where the clay is thickest, on the old lakebed in the east. On the rocky hills around the edges, it's much slower.
Here's the paradox. A city that was once a lake now suffers from two problems at once: floods, and not enough water.
As the city sinks, its drains lose their slope. Water that once flowed downhill out of the valley now has to be pumped uphill, out of a growing bowl.
So the city keeps building bigger tunnels. The Emisor Oriente, opened in 2019, is a drainage tunnel about sixty-two kilometers long.
Much of the city's wastewater flows north, out of the valley, into the state of Hidalgo, where it has long been used to irrigate farmland. A huge treatment plant at Atotonilco now cleans part of it.
Meanwhile, the wells can't supply everyone. So the city also brings water from outside the valley, through the Cutzamala System.
Its reservoirs lie more than a hundred kilometers to the west. The water has to be pumped uphill by more than a thousand meters to reach the city. That takes enormous amounts of electricity.
In 2024, after years of drought, the Cutzamala reservoirs fell to record lows, and many neighborhoods were left depending on water trucks.
And a large share of the water is lost before it arrives. Old pipes, many cracked by the sinking ground, leak an estimated forty percent of it.
That creates a vicious circle. Leaks mean more pumping. More pumping means more sinking. More sinking means more broken pipes, and more leaks.
Every rainy season, huge amounts of rain still fall on the city. But most of it is sent away through the drains, while wells pump water up from below.
During summer storms, heavy rain can fall in a short time, and streets on the lowest parts of the old lakebed still flood regularly.
So can Mexico City stop sinking? Not completely, at least not soon. But it can slow down.
The solutions are simple to describe and hard to do: fix the leaking pipes, pump less from the aquifer, and help more rain soak back into the ground.
Some projects already collect rainwater from rooftops, especially in poorer neighborhoods that get little water from the network.
Engineers are also studying ways to inject treated water back into the aquifer, to refill the giant sponge instead of only emptying it.
And in Xochimilco, farmers still grow vegetables and flowers on chinampas, using the same technique the Aztecs used centuries ago. Living with the water, not against it.
On part of the dry bed of Lake Texcoco, where a huge new airport was once planned, a large ecological park and wetlands are being restored.
Mexico City isn't alone. Other cities on soft ground that pump too much groundwater, like Jakarta in Indonesia, are sinking for the same reason.
That's why engineers around the world study Mexico City as a warning: this is what happens when a city takes much more water from the ground than nature can give back.
But nowhere is the story clearer than here, in a city that turned a lake into land, and is now paying the price, centimeter by centimeter.
So let's put the whole story together.
Mexico City sits in a closed valley that was once filled with lakes. Over thousands of years, those lakes left behind a thick layer of soft, water-filled clay.
Then the lake was drained, a giant city grew on top, and millions of people began pumping water from below. As the clay dries out, it compacts, and the city sinks.
The Aztecs built a city that lived with the lake. Five hundred years later, the lake is gone. But the ground still remembers it.

So why would a hundred million people squeeze into such a tiny space?

Take away the Nile, and Egypt is almost entirely desert. The Sahara covers nearly the whole country.
And look at the borders. Egypt's western and southern borders are almost perfectly straight lines. They were drawn in agreements between colonial powers along lines of longitude and latitude, and they could be drawn with a ruler because almost nobody lived there.
Cairo gets only around twenty-five millimeters of rain in an entire year. London gets more than twenty times that.
So picture Egypt as one hundred squares of land. Only about five of them are green enough to live on and farm.
Now picture the people. About ninety-five out of every hundred Egyptians live on those five squares.
That makes the Nile Valley one of the most crowded farming regions on Earth, with roughly two thousand people for every square kilometer of habitable land.
The answer is one river. The Nile, one of the longest rivers in the world, roughly six thousand six hundred kilometers from its farthest source to the Mediterranean.
Whether it's the longest river on Earth depends on who you ask. Some measurements put the Amazon ahead. It all comes down to where you decide a river really begins.
And here's the strange part. Most of Egypt's water doesn't fall in Egypt. It falls thousands of kilometers to the south, in the highlands of Ethiopia and around Lake Victoria.
Every summer, monsoon rains soak the Ethiopian Highlands. The water rushes down the Blue Nile, joins the White Nile at Khartoum, in Sudan, and flows north, straight through the Sahara.
That's why the Nile does something that looks impossible on a map. It crosses well over a thousand kilometers of desert without a single major river joining it, and without drying up.
It isn't easy. In the desert heat, the river loses an enormous amount of water to evaporation along the way. Only a river fed by a huge rainy region thousands of kilometers away could survive this journey.
And for thousands of years, the river did something even more important. Every summer, it flooded.
From around July to September, the river rose and spread across the valley. When the water pulled back, it left behind a layer of dark, fertile mud.
Ancient Egyptians called their country Kemet, the black land, after that mud. The desert around it was Deshret, the red land. Life on one side. Death on the other.
Their whole calendar followed the river. Three seasons: Akhet, the flood. Peret, the growing season. And Shemu, the harvest.
They even built nilometers, stone staircases and wells that measured how high the flood rose. The higher the water, the bigger the harvest, and the more tax farmers paid that year.
The Greek historian Herodotus summed it up about two and a half thousand years ago. Egypt, he wrote, is the gift of the river.
Now look where the great monuments are. The pyramids of Giza. The temples of Luxor. Aswan. Every one of them sits right next to the river.
The Nile was also Egypt's highway, with a clever trick. The current flows north, but the wind usually blows from the north. So boats drifted downstream with the current, then raised their sails and let the wind push them back upstream.
Even their writing reflects it. In hieroglyphs, the sign for traveling south shows a boat with its sail up. Traveling north, the sail is down.
And the flood may even help explain the pyramids. Many historians think that during the months when the fields were underwater, farmers were free to work on the pharaohs' building projects.
Reliable food from the river, and deserts on every side that kept invaders away. That combination is a big reason why one of the world's first great states appeared here, around five thousand years ago.

But the flood was never predictable. Too little water meant hunger. Too much could wash entire villages away.
So in the twentieth century, Egypt decided to control the river once and for all.
In 1970, the Aswan High Dam was completed. Behind it grew Lake Nasser, one of the largest artificial lakes in the world, around five hundred kilometers long.
The new lake was going to drown ancient monuments. So in the 1960s, an international campaign led by UNESCO cut the giant temples of Abu Simbel into more than a thousand blocks and rebuilt them on higher ground, about sixty-five meters up.
It wasn't the first dam at Aswan. A smaller one had been built by the British in 1902. But the new dam was on a completely different scale, and it changed Egypt forever.
The dam ended the floods. It stored water for dry years, and it produced huge amounts of electricity.
But there was a price. The fertile mud now gets trapped behind the dam. Farmers rely on fertilizers instead, and without fresh sediment, parts of the Nile Delta are slowly retreating into the sea.
Today, the delta and the valley hold one of the biggest cities on Earth. Greater Cairo is home to more than twenty million people.
There's one more thin strip where Egyptians cluster: along the Suez Canal. Opened in 1869, it links the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, and a big share of the world's trade passes through it, bringing Egypt billions of dollars every year.
But the delta faces a new threat. Much of it lies only a meter or two above the sea, and as sea levels rise and the land slowly sinks, salt water is creeping into some of Egypt's best farmland.
And Egypt keeps growing. Its population has multiplied roughly five times since 1950, and almost all of that growth has had to fit into the same narrow strip.
And the river itself is under pressure. Upstream, Ethiopia has built Africa's largest hydroelectric dam, the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, on the Blue Nile.
For Egypt, which gets more than ninety percent of its fresh water from the Nile, that's a huge worry. For Ethiopia, the dam means electricity for millions of people. Both countries see the river as a matter of survival.
Egypt is also trying to break out of the valley. East of Cairo, it's building a brand new capital city in the desert, and it's pumping Nile water to farm new land far from the river.
So why do ninety-five percent of Egyptians live on five percent of the land? Because in a country where it almost never rains, the Nile is the only water you can count on. For five thousand years, wherever the river goes, Egypt goes.

This is Istanbul. The only great city in the world that sits on two continents at once.
For more than two thousand years, it has been a prize that empires were willing to fight for, again and again.
Persians, Avars, Arabs, Bulgarians, Rus warriors, crusaders and Ottomans all came to its walls.
And the reason is right here: a narrow strip of water, just a few hundred meters wide at one point.
So why did every empire want this one city?
By the end of this video, you'll see how one strait, one harbor and one set of walls made this the most valuable piece of land between Europe and Asia.
Today, Istanbul is home to around fifteen and a half million people. It's by far the largest city in Turkey, and one of the largest in Europe.
To understand why it matters so much, zoom out. North of Istanbul is the Black Sea, a sea almost as big as Spain, surrounded by land.
The Black Sea has only one way out to the oceans. And that way runs straight through the middle of Istanbul.
Ships leaving the Black Sea must pass through the Bosphorus, then cross the small Sea of Marmara, then pass a second strait, the Dardanelles, before reaching the Aegean and the Mediterranean.
The Bosphorus is about thirty kilometers long. At its narrowest, it's only around seven hundred meters wide. You could almost shout across it.
Whoever holds this city holds the gate. They can let ships pass, charge them for passing, or stop them completely.
And the Bosphorus isn't only a sea route. It's also the shortest crossing between Europe and Asia. Armies and traders traveling by land have crossed here since ancient times.
So the city sits on a crossroads. Ships go north and south, between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. People and goods go east and west, between Europe and Asia.
Then there's the Golden Horn, a long, sheltered inlet on the European side. It's one of the best natural harbors in the whole Mediterranean world.
Add a peninsula with water on three sides, and you get a city that is easy to supply by sea and hard to attack by land. Perfect for trade, and perfect for defense.
Even the currents help the city. Huge rivers like the Danube pour fresh water into the Black Sea, so a strong surface current flows out through the Bosphorus. Ships sailing north have to fight against it.
So who first noticed what a perfect place this was? The answer involves a famous insult.
In the seventh century BC, Greek colonists from the city of Megara sailed here. First they founded Chalcedon, on the Asian shore. Some years later, others founded Byzantium, on the European side.
Look at the two sites. Chalcedon had no great harbor. Byzantium had the Golden Horn, and a peninsula that was much easier to defend.
According to the Greek historian Herodotus, a Persian general later said the people of Chalcedon must have been blind, to settle there when the better site was right in front of them.
Byzantium grew rich from the strait. In ancient times, huge amounts of grain from the lands around the Black Sea passed through the Bosphorus on their way to the cities of Greece.
Athens, in particular, depended on that grain. Whoever controlled the strait could feed Athens, or starve it.
So for centuries, Byzantium was fought over by Persians, Athenians, Spartans and Macedonians. Even as a small Greek town, it was already a prize.
In 340 BC, Philip of Macedon, the father of Alexander the Great, besieged Byzantium. Even he failed to take it.
But its real rise came when a Roman emperor decided that this was the place to build the new center of the world.
By the early three hundreds AD, the Roman Empire's main threats were in the east and along the Danube. The city of Rome was simply too far away.
So the emperor Constantine rebuilt Byzantium as his new capital. In the year 330, it was dedicated as New Rome. People soon called it Constantinople, the city of Constantine.
When the western half of the Roman Empire collapsed in the four hundreds, the eastern half survived, ruled from Constantinople. Historians call it the Byzantine Empire, but its people simply called themselves Romans.
To protect the city, in the early four hundreds, the emperor Theodosius the Second built a new line of walls across the peninsula, from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn.
On the land side, there was only one short front to defend, about six kilometers long. On every other side, the city was protected by water.
And it wasn't one wall. Attackers first faced a wide moat, then an outer wall, then a taller inner wall with nearly a hundred towers.
Those walls would protect the city for about a thousand years. Many historians consider them the most successful defensive walls ever built.
Behind them, Constantinople became the largest and richest city in Europe. In the five hundreds, it may have had around half a million people.
Its gold coin, the solidus, was trusted from Western Europe to Persia, and stayed remarkably stable for centuries. Some historians call it the dollar of the Middle Ages.
Crowds of tens of thousands watched chariot races in the Hippodrome. In 532, riots between rival racing fans almost overthrew the emperor Justinian.
In 537, the emperor Justinian completed Hagia Sophia. For nearly a thousand years, it was the largest cathedral in the world.
A huge city, an enormous treasury and control of the busiest crossroads in the world. Now every enemy of the empire knew exactly where to aim.
In 626, the city was attacked from both sides at once. The Avars came from the European side, while a Persian army waited across the Bosphorus in Asia.
But the two armies needed to join up. And the Byzantine fleet controlled the strait between them. The Persians never crossed, and the siege failed.
A few decades later, the armies of the new Arab caliphate arrived. They attacked the city in the 670s, and again in 717, with a huge army and fleet.
The Byzantines had a secret weapon: Greek fire, a liquid that kept burning even on water. It was sprayed onto enemy ships, and its exact recipe is still a mystery today.
The great siege of 717 lasted about a year. A harsh winter, disease, and attacks on its fleet forced the Arab army to retreat.
Then, in 860, a fleet of Rus warriors sailed down the rivers of Eastern Europe and across the Black Sea to raid the city's suburbs. The Rus would return several times.
Bulgarian rulers marched to the walls more than once as well. Again and again, the city survived. The walls held, and food kept arriving by sea.
That was the secret. To take Constantinople, an enemy had to break the land walls and control the sea at the same time. For centuries, no one could do both.
The first foreign army to break in didn't come from the east at all. It came from Western Europe, and it was supposed to be on the same side.
In 1204, the Fourth Crusade, which had set out to fight in the Holy Land, turned against Constantinople instead. The crusaders broke in from the Golden Horn and sacked the city.
Venice, which had supplied the crusaders' ships, took a large share of the treasures. The famous bronze horses that stand in St. Mark's Basilica in Venice were taken from Constantinople.
Italian merchants moved in for good. Genoa ran its own walled trading colony at Galata, across the Golden Horn. Its Galata Tower, built in 1348, still stands today.
The Byzantines retook their city in 1261. But it never recovered its old wealth. And a new power was rising right next door.
By the 1400s, the Ottoman Turks had conquered almost everything around the city, in Asia and in Europe. Constantinople was an island of Roman rule surrounded by Ottoman land.
Its population had collapsed too. From perhaps half a million people at its height, it now had maybe fifty thousand.
In 1453, the young sultan Mehmed the Second, only twenty-one years old, decided to take it. He had already built a fortress on the Bosphorus to cut off help from the Black Sea.
His army was many times larger than the defenders, who numbered perhaps seven or eight thousand. And he brought something new: enormous cannons.
One of them was built by a Hungarian engineer called Orban. It could fire stone balls weighing hundreds of kilograms, and it took dozens of oxen to move it.
The defenders had blocked the entrance to the Golden Horn with a huge chain stretched across the water. So Mehmed did something astonishing.
He had dozens of ships dragged overland, across the hills behind Galata, and launched them into the Golden Horn, behind the chain.
After about fifty-three days of siege and bombardment, on the twenty-ninth of May, 1453, the Ottoman army broke through the walls. The last Byzantine emperor died in the fighting.
After almost eleven hundred years, the Roman Empire that Constantine had moved here was gone.
Many historians use 1453 to mark the end of the Middle Ages. Greek scholars who fled to Italy carried ancient texts with them, adding fuel to the Renaissance.
Mehmed made the city his capital. Hagia Sophia became a mosque, and the city was rebuilt and filled again with people brought in from across the empire.
For the Ottomans, the city was the key to everything. From here, they could rule both their European and Asian lands, and control all trade in and out of the Black Sea.

Under the Ottomans, the city grew again. In the 1500s, it was probably the largest city in Europe, filled with mosques, markets and palaces.
The great architect Mimar Sinan built the Süleymaniye Mosque in the 1550s on one of the city's hills, and the sultans ruled from the Topkapı Palace, overlooking the meeting of the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn.
But as the Ottoman Empire weakened, a new power looked at the straits with great interest. Russia.
Russia's southern ports were on the Black Sea. Every Russian warship and grain ship heading for the Mediterranean had to pass through Istanbul, under the guns of another empire.
Some Russian thinkers even called the city Tsargrad, the city of the emperor, and dreamed of taking it back for Orthodox Christianity.
Britain and France saw the danger. If Russia controlled the straits, its fleet could sail freely into the Mediterranean, close to their trade routes. So they often backed the Ottomans to keep Russia out.
That rivalry was one of the causes of the Crimean War in the 1850s. And in the First World War, the straits were at the center of the fighting again.
In 1915, British and French forces tried to force their way through the Dardanelles and reach Istanbul. The naval attack failed, and the landings at Gallipoli turned into a long, costly campaign before the Allies withdrew.
In secret, the Allies had even agreed that Russia would get the city after the war. But Russia's revolution in 1917 ended that plan.
After the war, Allied troops occupied Istanbul until 1923. When the Republic of Turkey was founded that year, its capital became Ankara, far inland.
Part of the reason was the lesson of history. A capital on the strait was rich, but it was also exposed to every fleet that could reach it.
In 1936, the Montreux Convention set the rules for the straits. Turkey controls them. Merchant ships can pass freely in peacetime, but warships face strict limits.
That treaty is still in force today. And in times of war in the region, it has made Turkey's control of the straits as important as ever.
Today, tens of thousands of ships pass through the Bosphorus every year, many of them tankers and bulk carriers, sailing right past the city's homes and mosques.
Grain from Ukraine and Russia, and oil from the Black Sea region, still travel this route, just as grain did in the days of ancient Athens.
And the old land bridge is busier than ever. The first bridge across the Bosphorus opened in 1973. Today there are three bridges, a rail tunnel opened in 2013, and a road tunnel opened in 2016.
Every day, huge numbers of people commute between Europe and Asia. For them, changing continents is just the trip to work.
The city has grown enormously. Around one million people lived here in 1950. Today it's more than fifteen times that.
Even its name tells the story of a crossroads. Istanbul most likely comes from a Greek phrase meaning to the city. It became the city's only official name in 1930.
There's one danger the walls can't stop. Istanbul lies close to a major earthquake fault under the Sea of Marmara, and the city is working to strengthen its buildings.
But the geography that made the city great hasn't changed in thousands of years. And it isn't going to.
So let's put the whole story together.
Istanbul controls the only sea route between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, and the shortest crossing between Europe and Asia. It has one of the best natural harbors in the region.
That made it rich, and that made it a target. Its walls and its fleets held off attackers for a thousand years, until cannons and ships dragged over land finally broke through.
That's why Istanbul was the prize every empire wanted. Not because of its gold, or its palaces, but because of where it stands: on a narrow strip of water between two seas and two continents.

This is Australia. A country the size of a continent.
It's almost as big as the mainland United States. But while the United States has more than three hundred million people, Australia has only around twenty-seven million.
And almost all of them live here, along the edges. Mostly in the east and the south-east, and in one corner of the west.
The middle, an area bigger than most countries on Earth, is almost completely empty.
So why does almost no one live in the middle of Australia?
By the end of this video, you'll see how sinking air, a wall of mountains, ancient soil and a few brave and stubborn people drew the map of where Australians live.
Australia covers around seven point seven million square kilometers. It's the sixth largest country in the world, and the only one that covers an entire continent.
To get a sense of the scale, put it over Europe. From Perth to Sydney is almost as far as from Madrid to Moscow.
Spread evenly, Australia would have only about three and a half people per square kilometer. That's one of the lowest densities of any country on Earth.
Compare that with the United Kingdom, at around two hundred and eighty, or the United States, at around thirty-five.
But just like in other giant countries, the average is misleading. Because Australians are not spread out at all. They're packed together.
About two out of every three Australians live in just a handful of capital cities.
Sydney and Melbourne, with around five million people each. Then Brisbane, Perth and Adelaide. Every one of them is on the coast.
And more than eight in ten Australians live within about fifty kilometers of the sea.
Now flip it around. The areas officially classed as remote or very remote cover most of the country, but they are home to only about two percent of the people.
So the question is not why Australia has few people. It's why almost none of them live in the heart of their own country.
Let's go to the middle. Right near the center of the continent is the town of Alice Springs, with around twenty-five thousand people.
The nearest big city is Adelaide, around one thousand three hundred kilometers away in a straight line. Darwin is about as far in the other direction.
Around four hundred and fifty kilometers away by road is Uluru, the giant sandstone rock that rises about three hundred and fifty meters above the plain.
It is sacred to the Anangu, the Aboriginal traditional owners of the land, and since 2019, climbing it has been banned.
All around it is the outback. A huge, dry interior made of deserts, rocky ranges, scrubland and salt lakes.
The largest of these deserts, the Great Victoria Desert, covers around three hundred and fifty thousand square kilometers on its own. That's bigger than Italy.
The Northern Territory, which includes much of the center, is more than twice the size of France. But it has only around a quarter of a million people.
And in the south lies Lake Eyre, or Kati Thanda. At about fifteen meters below sea level, it's the lowest point in Australia, and the largest lake on the continent.
Except most of the time, it isn't a lake at all. It's a crust of dry salt. It has filled completely only a handful of times since records began.
When it does fill, after big rains far to the north, the desert comes alive. Huge flocks of pelicans and other waterbirds fly in from the coast, hundreds of kilometers away.
And the heat is extreme. Australia's official record is around fifty point seven degrees Celsius, measured in the outback town of Oodnadatta in 1960, and equalled on the west coast in 2022.
In the 1920s, the mining town of Marble Bar recorded one hundred and sixty days in a row above thirty-seven point eight degrees. That's still a world record.
So yes, the outback is hot. But heat alone doesn't empty a continent. Plenty of hot places are crowded. The real problem is something else.
The real problem is water. Apart from Antarctica, Australia is the driest continent on Earth.
Around seventy percent of the mainland is classed as arid or semi-arid. That means very little rain, and in many years, almost none.
The first reason is where Australia sits. Look at this band of latitude, around thirty degrees south. It runs right through the middle of the continent.
Near the equator, warm air rises and drops its rain. Then that air flows away from the equator, cools, and sinks again around thirty degrees north and south. Sinking air is dry, and it stops clouds from forming.
That's the same reason the Sahara, the Arabian Desert and the Kalahari exist. Australia simply has a huge piece of land sitting right in that dry belt.
The second reason is a wall. Along the east coast runs the Great Dividing Range, stretching for more than three thousand kilometers.
Moist winds from the Pacific hit those mountains and rise. As the air cools, it drops its rain on the coastal side. By the time it crosses to the inland side, much of the moisture is gone.
That's why the green, fertile strip is in the east. It's the wet side of the mountains. And it's exactly where most Australians live today.
The third reason is the shape of the land itself. Australia is the flattest continent. Its highest mountain, Mount Kosciuszko, is only about two thousand two hundred meters high.
Without high mountains in the interior, there is little to push air upward and squeeze rain out of it. The clouds just pass over.
Australia's biggest river system, the Murray and the Darling, drains around a seventh of the continent. It's one of the main farming regions of the country.
But even this river can slow to a trickle in drought, and in some dry years its mouth has needed dredging to stay open to the sea.
Under the outback, there's a surprise. The Great Artesian Basin, one of the largest underground water reserves in the world, lies beneath about a fifth of Australia.
It keeps cattle and some towns alive. But much of that water is ancient, slowly refilled, and often too salty or too deep to support big farms or cities.
And then there's the soil. Australia is one of the oldest landmasses on Earth. Its rocks have been weathered for hundreds of millions of years, with few volcanoes or glaciers to bring fresh minerals.
So the middle of Australia has three problems at once. Sinking dry air, mountains that steal the rain, and old soils that are poor in nutrients.
And yet, people have lived in the outback for an extraordinarily long time.
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived in Australia for at least fifty thousand years, and possibly more than sixty-five thousand. It's one of the oldest continuous cultures on Earth.
Desert peoples survived in the harshest parts of the continent with knowledge passed down over countless generations: where to find water, when to move, and what could be eaten.
Rock holes, soaks and springs were remembered and shared through stories, songs and law, sometimes connecting places hundreds of kilometers apart.
But notice something. Desert groups were small, and they moved across very large areas. The land could support people, but not many in one place.
Aboriginal people also shaped the land itself, using carefully controlled fires to renew plants and attract animals. Today, this cultural burning is again being used to help manage the bush.
Even before Europeans arrived, the population was much denser along the wetter coasts and rivers, like the Murray, than in the deep desert.
So the pattern we see today is ancient. Water has always decided how many people the land could hold. And in the middle, the answer has always been: very few.
In 1788, the First Fleet of eleven British ships arrived at Sydney Cove, carrying around seven hundred and fifty convicts, plus soldiers, officials and their families.
Over the next decades, more colonies were founded. Each one had its own capital, and every capital was a port. Hobart, Brisbane, Melbourne, Perth, Adelaide.
That made sense. Everything came and went by sea: people, supplies, wool and gold. Each colony was more connected to London than to its neighbors.
In fact, the colonies even built their railways with different track widths. Trains from one colony often couldn't run on the tracks of the next.
It wasn't until 1970 that a train could run all the way from Sydney to Perth on one standard track.
Meanwhile, the interior remained a mystery to the colonists. Some even believed there might be a great inland sea in the middle of the continent.
In 1860, the Burke and Wills expedition set out from Melbourne to cross the continent from south to north. They reached the tidal channels near the Gulf of Carpentaria, but on the way back, both leaders died.
A year later, John McDouall Stuart crossed from Adelaide to the north coast and returned alive. His route became the path of one of the great engineering projects of the century.
In 1872, the Overland Telegraph Line was finished. About three thousand two hundred kilometers of wire from Adelaide to Darwin, where it joined an undersea cable to Asia and on to Britain.
News from London that once took weeks by ship now arrived in hours. And along the line, small repeater stations were built in the desert to boost the signal.
One of those stations was built by a waterhole in the center of the continent. The town that grew around it is Alice Springs.
So the most famous town in the outback didn't grow because the land was rich. It grew because a wire needed to cross it.

Of course, settlers did try to farm further inland. And one man tried to warn them where to stop.
In 1865, after a severe drought, George Goyder, the surveyor general of South Australia, traveled the colony and drew a line on the map. North of it, he said, rainfall was too unreliable for growing crops.
Then came a run of unusually wet years in the 1870s. Many people decided Goyder had been wrong, and farms and towns spread north of his line.
When the dry years returned, the crops failed. Today you can still find abandoned stone farmhouses beyond the line, and it is still used as a guide for where cropping is risky.
To move goods across the desert, the colonists brought in camels, along with cameleers mostly from what are now Afghanistan, Pakistan and India.
When trucks and railways replaced them, many camels were released. Today Australia has hundreds of thousands of wild camels, one of the largest wild camel populations in the world.
The cameleers are remembered in the name of a famous train, the Ghan, which today runs from Adelaide through Alice Springs to Darwin. The line only reached Darwin in 2004.
Where crops failed, cattle worked. But on dry land, each animal needs a huge area of grazing. So the outback is divided into cattle stations of incredible size.
The largest, Anna Creek Station in South Australia, covers around twenty-four thousand square kilometers. That's bigger than the whole of Israel, run by just a small team of people.
Then there's mining. In the 1910s, opal was found at Coober Pedy. Today, Australia produces most of the world's precious opal.
Summer days in Coober Pedy are so hot that many residents live underground, in homes dug into the hillside called dugouts, where the temperature stays comfortable all year.
Every one of these places shows the same thing. People can live in the outback. But only with a special reason, and only in small numbers.
So what's it like to live there now? The biggest challenge is distance. The nearest hospital, school or supermarket can be hundreds of kilometers away.
In 1928, a minister called John Flynn started an aerial medical service. Today, the Royal Flying Doctor Service flies doctors and nurses to patients across the outback.
And from 1951, children on remote stations could attend the School of the Air, first with lessons over two-way radio, and today over the internet.
The outback is also where much of Australia's wealth comes from. Huge iron ore mines in the Pilbara, in the north-west, have helped make Australia the world's largest iron ore exporter.
But many miners don't live there. They fly in from Perth for a couple of weeks of long shifts, then fly home again. The mines grow, but the towns don't.
The far north is different again. It's tropical, not desert. But almost all of its rain falls in a few months of the wet season, cutting off roads, followed by months with almost no rain at all.
Even the largest inland city in Australia is Canberra, the national capital, with around four hundred and seventy thousand people. And it's only about one hundred and fifty kilometers from the sea.
Canberra was a compromise. In the early 1900s, Sydney and Melbourne couldn't agree which should be the capital, so a new city was built between them.
Looking ahead, the outback's sunshine is among the strongest on Earth, and there are plans to use it for huge solar farms. But solar panels, like mines, need very few people.
And climate change may make the dry interior even harder, with more intense heat waves and droughts.
So let's put the whole story together.
The middle of Australia sits in the same dry belt as the Sahara. Mountains in the east catch the rain before it can travel inland. And the ancient soils are poor.
The colonies grew as ports facing the sea, and every attempt to settle the interior ran into the same limit: water.
That's why almost no one lives in the middle of Australia. Not because no one tried, but because the land and the climate have always decided how many people it can hold.
